## What ORCID Actually Is

ORCID — Open Researcher and Contributor ID — is a persistent 16-digit identifier that uniquely distinguishes you from every other researcher in the world, regardless of how common your name is, how many institutions you have worked at, or how many variations of your name appear in publications.

It looks like this: `0000-0001-2345-6789`

It is free. It is permanent. It is increasingly mandatory for grant applications, journal submissions, and institutional reporting. And most researchers have set theirs up incorrectly — or not at all.

## The Disambiguation Problem

Academic publishing has a fundamental attribution problem. Common names create citation collisions. Name changes after marriage or for cultural reasons fragment publication records. Transliteration differences across alphabets create phantom identities. A single researcher may appear as five different "authors" across five different databases.

ORCID solves this by anchoring your entire scholarly output to a single, persistent, institutional-independent identifier. It does not matter if you publish as "A. B. Smith," "Anna Smith," or "A. Smith" — your ORCID iD connects them all.

## Building Your ORCID Profile Correctly

### Step 1 — Create and verify your account

Go to orcid.org and create your profile. Use your primary professional email. Verify your email address immediately — unverified accounts have limited integration capabilities.

### Step 2 — Set your visibility correctly

ORCID has three visibility levels for each section: **Everyone**, **Trusted parties**, and **Only me**.

For maximum visibility:
- Set your name and professional details to **Everyone**
- Set your works (publications) to **Everyone** or **Trusted parties** at minimum
- Keep sensitive information (personal address, private email) at **Only me**

### Step 3 — Import your works from trusted sources

Do not manually enter publications one by one — this creates errors and duplication. Instead, use ORCID's direct import tools:

- **Crossref Metadata Search** — imports DOI-linked publications automatically
- **Scopus to ORCID** — for papers indexed in Scopus
- **Europe PubMed Central** — for life science publications
- **DataCite** — for datasets and research data

After each import, **review carefully**. Remove any works that belong to another researcher with a similar name. This disambiguation step is critical.

### Step 4 — Connect your ORCID to your institutional profile

Most research institutions now support ORCID integration. When connected, your institution can automatically push new publications to your ORCID profile and verify your affiliation. Contact your library or research office to activate this.

### Step 5 — Add it everywhere

Your ORCID iD should appear:
- In your email signature
- On your institutional profile page
- In your Google Scholar profile
- On every manuscript you submit (in the author information section)
- On your LinkedIn and ResearchGate profiles
- In every grant application

## ORCID and the Scholarly Ecosystem

ORCID integrates with over 1,200 member organisations — including Elsevier, Springer Nature, Wiley, the Wellcome Trust, NIH, and hundreds of universities. When you authenticate with ORCID on these platforms, your identity is verified, your publications are auto-populated, and your contributions are tracked accurately across the entire system.

This is not a passive benefit. It is an active visibility infrastructure. Researchers with complete, well-maintained ORCID profiles appear more credibly in systematic reviews, are easier to contact for collaboration, and experience fewer attribution errors over the course of their careers.

## The ORCID Maintenance Habit

An ORCID profile is not a one-time setup. It requires a quarterly maintenance habit:

1. Import any new publications using trusted sources
2. Review and remove any mis-attributed works
3. Update your current institutional affiliation if it has changed
4. Add new grants, datasets, and peer review activities
5. Confirm your profile visibility settings remain correct

Fifteen minutes every three months is sufficient. The compounding effect over a research career is significant.
